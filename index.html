01

How I decide what to build

Five things I've come to believe, each one paid for by a decision that went well or badly.

Automate the step people skip, not the step people hate.

The slow step is visible and everyone is already optimising it. The valuable step is the one quietly skipped because doing it properly is too painful — in trade, that's the pre-filing compliance check. Nobody runs it, everybody pays for it at the border, and the cost surfaces somewhere else entirely: demurrage, amendment fees, late-filing penalties. That gap between where the pain is felt and where it was caused is usually where the product is.

Decide what "wrong" costs before you decide what "good" looks like.

A model at 95% is excellent for drafting a document and unacceptable for screening a sanctioned party. Same accuracy, opposite verdict, because the cost of the missing 5% is different by orders of magnitude. I set the failure cost first and let the accuracy bar, the review design and the human-in-the-loop fall out of it — not the other way round.

Ship the boring half first.

Document generation before document intelligence. Generation is unglamorous, trivially measurable, and removes the single largest block of manual work on day one. That's what buys the organisational credit to attempt the hard, uncertain thing next. Teams that lead with the ambitious half tend to spend their credibility before they've earned any.

A number without a denominator is a story.

"93% faster" means nothing until you say: across which document types, at what daily volume, measured against whose baseline, over what window, and what the figure excludes. I'd rather present a smaller number I can defend in a room full of sceptics than a larger one that falls apart on the second question.

Regulated users don't want autonomy. They want defensibility.

A customs broker is not asking the system to decide for them. They're asking for something they can put in front of an auditor eighteen months later and explain — which list, which version, which match, which person signed. In this domain the audit trail isn't a compliance checkbox bolted on at the end; it's the product. Design it before the interface.

02

Trade Compliance Engine

In build · MVP demo-ready Kale Logistics AI agents · 2025–26

The situation

A single international shipment has to satisfy roughly a dozen regulatory bodies — customs, foreign trade, port and airport authorities, sector agencies, sanctions regimes, tax — each with its own filings, formats and timelines. Today that check is done by hand or skipped entirely. So problems surface only once the shipment is already stopped at the border, which is the most expensive possible moment to find them.

How I framed it

The obvious framing is "automate customs filing." I think that's wrong. Filing isn't where the loss is — filing is fast. The loss is that compliance gets verified at the authority: the cargo is already at the border, demurrage is accruing, and the fix needs a person who happens to know that one specific rule.

So the product isn't a filing tool. It's a verdict that arrives before the authority does.

Every architectural decision after that follows from that single sentence — which checks exist, how fast they must return, and who is accountable for the answer.

The decision I'd defend hardest

Twenty-one agents, not one model.

The instinct is a single large prompt over the whole document set. I argued for decomposition instead, on product grounds rather than engineering ones:

  • Independent failure domains. A wrong HS classification and a missed sanctions match are different incidents, with different owners and different remediation. Collapse them into one output and neither is attributable — so neither gets fixed.
  • Different data half-lives. Sanctions and watchlists move daily; HS tariff schedules annually; FTA rules of origin by treaty. A monolith forces the slowest-moving component to set the refresh cadence for everything.
  • Evaluation only works on isolated checks. Twenty-one agents means twenty-one eval sets and twenty-one accuracy targets you can actually argue about. One prompt means one unmeasurable blob, and "it feels better" as your only quality signal.
  • Latency. Independent checks run in parallel, so a full verdict returns in seconds. A sequential chain over the same checks does not, and a check that's slower than a phone call to a broker doesn't get used.

What the engine is made of

Five disciplines. The grouping is by who owns the failure, not by what's technically similar.

The trade-off I spent the most time on

Precision versus recall in party screening — and why it isn't symmetric.

Fuzzy-matching a party name against eighty-five-plus lists forces a choice, and the two errors are not remotely equal:

  • A false negative — a sanctioned party clears — is a regulatory incident with legal exposure. Effectively unbounded cost.
  • A false positive — a clean party gets flagged — costs an analyst a couple of minutes. Bounded, and it gets cheaper every time you improve the review screen.

So: tune hard toward recall, and spend the engineering budget on making review cheap rather than on making the flag rare. That's what produced the verdict ladder — three states instead of two.

Clear
No action needed. Evidence and list versions retained anyway, because the auditor's question comes later.
Review
A person decides. The system shows the candidate match, which list it came from, and exactly what differs — so the decision takes seconds, not a research session.
Match
Blocked, with an escalation path. The system never resolves this one silently.

Two states — pass or fail — would have forced the model to make a judgement it isn't entitled to make. Three states let it do the thing it's genuinely good at, which is narrowing the field, and leave the accountable call with the person who has to sign their name to it.

How I'd know it's working

Not "accuracy." A single accuracy figure on a dashboard hides the failure mode that actually matters here. The pair I'd hold it to:

  • Review load per hundred shipments — should fall over time as matching improves.
  • Escaped-miss rate — should stay at zero, full stop.

You need both, because a system can drive review load down simply by getting bolder, and that looks like progress right up until it isn't. Watching the two together is the only way to tell improvement from erosion.

How I sized the opportunity

Bottom-up from declaration volume rather than top-down from an analyst's market figure, because the unit that actually bills here is a document, not a seat:

  • United States — roughly 96M import and export declarations a year, handled by about 11,000 customs brokers and freight forwarders. (CBP trade statistics; trade.gov)
  • European Union — roughly 311M declarations, about 19,000 brokers. (Verified Market Research; CLECAT)
  • Trade-management software globally — around $2.7B, with North America at roughly 36–38%. (Coherent Market Insights)

Two figures shaped the framing more than the market size did: roughly 2,000+ additions to sanctions designations every year, and $4.6B+ in AML, KYC and sanctions fines issued in 2024. The first says any static rules engine decays from the day it ships. The second says the buyer already has a budget line for this failure — which is a much easier sale than creating one.

Where it honestly stands

The engine and agent architecture are built and demo-ready. Pricing and pilot scoping with early prospects are in progress. It has not yet run at production volume with paying customers, and the accuracy targets are targets rather than measured production results. I'd rather say that plainly than let a portfolio imply otherwise.

Maritime Single Window

Live · 20 ports Kale Logistics 0 → 1 · 2024–26

The situation

A single vessel call touches about a dozen agencies — customs, port authority, immigration, health, terminal operators — each with its own form, format and deadline. Shipping agents re-key the same twenty facts into every one of them, under time pressure, with a berth waiting.

How I framed it

The obvious product is a portal that submits to every agency. But submission is the easy half. The hard half is that no two agencies agree on what a vessel call even is — different field names, different granularity, different mandatory sets for the same real-world fact.

So the asset isn't the portal. It's a canonical model every agency's format can be derived from.

Get that right and each new integration is a configuration exercise. Get it wrong and every new port is a rebuild wearing a different name.

What I chose not to build

  • Not agency-by-agency integrations first. Tempting, because each one demos beautifully. But it produces N bespoke pipelines and no reusable asset. I built the canonical model against one agency, proved derivation actually worked, and only then let integrations become a repeatable motion.
  • Not a mobile app. Requested repeatedly. Shipping agents work at a desk, in a browser, with a dozen tabs open, moving data between systems. A phone makes that job measurably worse, and building one would have been responsiveness to volume rather than to need.
  • Not analytics dashboards, for a long time. Every stakeholder asked. Nobody would have used them before there was enough clean data to be worth looking at — and building them early would have frozen the data model at exactly the moment it still needed to move.

Why Port Klang first

Deliberately the hardest corridor rather than the easiest one. Highest volume, most complex agency mix. A canonical model validated on a simple port would have needed rebuilding at the first complicated one; a model that survives Klang generalises across the region. The price is a slower first launch — which is the right trade when the thing you're building is a model rather than a feature.

The metric that actually mattered

"Ports live" is a sales number. It tells you nothing about whether the product is getting better. The product number was derivation coverage: what share of a new port's mandatory fields can be produced from the canonical model without a schema change.

That's the only figure that makes port twenty-one cheaper than port one. It's the number I'd take into a roadmap review, and the one I'd use to argue against a feature that quietly forces a schema fork.

What I got wrong

I underweighted change management. The system was correct well before the agents trusted it — and a correct output that a user won't sign is worth precisely nothing. The fix was mundane: a side-by-side view of the hand-filled form against the generated one, so an agent could verify at a glance instead of re-doing the work to check our answer. It should have been in the first release.

I now treat "how does this user verify the output without redoing the work?" as part of the definition of done rather than a follow-up ticket. It's the single most useful habit I picked up from this project.

Three more, briefly

Shipped at Kale. Each one here for the framing rather than the headline number.

AI document generation

RAG + LLM · ~200 docs/day · 20+ types

Turnaround went from about 30 minutes to under 2 — a 93% cut, and the largest single block of manual effort for port operations teams.

How that was measured  Baseline timed against the manual drafting path per document type, then weighted by the actual daily mix rather than cherry-picking the slowest type. What the figure excludes: the review time a human still spends afterwards. So the honest claim is 93% off drafting, not 93% off the process — and I say it that way in rooms where someone will check.

NLP support assistant

Tickets 250/mo → under 20 · 92% cut

The framing  Tier-1 volume wasn't a support problem. It was a product-clarity problem wearing a support costume — roughly the same handful of questions, asked over and over, because the product never made those answers obvious.

Deflection was the headline. The genuinely useful output was the ranked list of what the interface had failed to communicate, which went straight into the roadmap. A chatbot that permanently absorbs the same question is a product debt you've agreed to service forever.

Cargo forecasting & resource planning

Demand visibility for warehouse ops

The framing  Warehouses don't fail because they lack capacity. They fail because they find out about a demand spike the morning it arrives, when every lever worth pulling needs more notice than that.

So the product isn't a better forecast — it's a longer lead time on a good-enough one. Horizon beats precision here, and that reordering changed what we built and what we stopped tuning.

Payments & invoicing

Multi-party settlement · +70% transaction transparency

End-to-end automation of settlement workflows across multiple parties, where the hard part is never the payment rail — it's agreeing who owes what, to whom, on whose version of the truth.

Before this: what 32M a day teaches you

Mindgate Solutions UPI · NPCI · HDFC Bank 2023–24

What I did

  • Drove requirements and stakeholder alignment for the UPI 6.0 rollout at HDFC Bank, coordinating product, engineering and client teams against NPCI guidelines, on schedule.
  • Built a competitive transaction-analytics framework comparing HDFC's UPI performance against peer banks, surfacing the business and technical drivers behind the gaps for executive decisions.
  • Owned reconciliation across 32M+ data points per day spanning 8 NPCI settlement cycles — accuracy, auditability and regulatory compliance at production scale.
  • Found and cleared pipeline bottlenecks in SQL and Kafka, taking end-to-end processing from 4+ hours to under 45 minutes (81%) and making near-real-time reconciliation possible.

What it actually taught me

At 32 million transactions a day you don't manage the average, you manage the tail. Reconciliation never fails on the typical transaction — it fails on the fraction of a percent that arrive late, arrive twice, or arrive against a settlement cycle that has already closed. Every hour of real work was spent there.

Spec the failure modes before the happy path. The happy path will get built regardless.

It's the same instinct I now apply to AI outputs. The interesting question was never what the system does when it's right.

What I'm still working out

Three things I don't have a settled answer to. I'd rather show the edge of what I know than pretend it isn't there.

Keeping an eval honest once users adapt to it.

I can build an offline eval set and defend an accuracy target. What I'm less sure about is what happens six months in, when users have quietly learned the system's blind spots and started routing around them — at which point the eval set stops resembling live traffic and the metric keeps looking fine. I don't have a clean method for this yet.

Pricing AI that's priced per document.

Per-seat pricing fits a product whose cost scales with users. It fits badly when cost scales with documents and value scales with penalties avoided — a customer with fewer, riskier shipments gets far more value from fewer units. I've argued both sides of per-document versus outcome-linked pricing and I'm not convinced the industry has converged either.

Telling a domain expert's judgement from their workaround.

In trade compliance the SME is usually right, and occasionally defending a workaround they invented years ago to survive a system that no longer exists. Both sound identical in a requirements session. I've got heuristics — ask for the last three times it mattered, ask who else does it this way — but it's still mostly judgement rather than method, and I get it wrong sometimes.
